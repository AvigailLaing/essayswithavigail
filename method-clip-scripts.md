# Five clips for the Yes Pile stages

Roughly 30 seconds each. Every line here is built from how you already say this
on calls, so it should read like something you'd say, not something you'd read.
The bolded sentence is the one to land on.

Same setup as your sessions: Golden Gate background, looking at the camera. One
take each is fine, and it is better if it is not perfect.

---

## 01 Inventory
*What do I actually have to write about?*

The thing I tell every student on the first call is that the event doesn't have
to be big. People read the challenge prompt and think colleges want the
traumatic one, so they go looking for the worst thing that's ever happened to
them, and that isn't what's being scored. I've read over a thousand of these
now, and what actually matters is the growth, so what I'm listening for is a
moment where you got something wrong, or where there's a clear before and after
in the way you think. **You don't pick a topic from a list of topics. You pick
it from a list of moments.**

---

## 02 Angle
*Which idea can carry 650 words, and what is it about?*

Once we have the moment, the question I ask is what it's actually about,
because a story on its own isn't an argument yet. The test I use is whether
someone else could technically write this, and if they could, we're not done.
One of the most effective things you can do is take the lesson you're pulling
out of your moment and prove that it's bigger than just you, that there's a
real reason the person reading it should care. **That's the difference between
a nice story and an essay that's arguing something.**

---

## 03 Architecture
*What goes in each paragraph?*

Before you write a single word, I want you to know what every paragraph is
doing. Most of the rewriting students do isn't a writing problem, it's that
they started typing without a plan and then spent three weeks moving pieces
around hoping it would click. So we plan the transitions first, what each
paragraph hands to the next one and how the idea gets more complex as you go.
**You start from the outline and then you write the draft, and the draft comes
out in a fraction of the time.**

---

## 04 Evidence
*Am I showing this, or just claiming it?*

This is the pass where we go line by line, and honestly it's where most of the
work happens. Anywhere you've told me something about yourself, we replace it
with a moment that proves it, because saying you're perceptive or resilient
does nothing when anyone can type that sentence. I want the detail only you
would know. **It's also the pass that makes an essay read like a person instead
of a machine, which matters more now than it ever has.**

---

## 05 Screen
*Is this actually finished?*

The last thing we do is read it the way the person on the other end is going to
read it. They're going through hundreds of these, so when they hit a huge block
of text it gets really easy to start skimming instead of actually reading, and
you lose them in the part you worked hardest on. We keep the paragraphs tight,
because every new paragraph gives them a reset and re-sparks their attention.
**Then you submit knowing it's done, instead of hoping.**
